# The state of agent commerce, and an experiment

Draft 1, 23 November 2026. Not published. Every figure has its date and source at the end. Where there is no good number, we say so.

## In short

AI agents can now shop. Almost nobody lets them. The growth figures are enormous and the real numbers are tiny, and both are true at once.

We think the thing holding it back is trust, and that trust will arrive the day people can see what their agent saved them. We think those savings can come from money that brands pay today just to reach their own customers. This paper sets out where things stand, what we believe, and the experiment we are running in public to find out if we are right.

## Where things stand

### 1. The growth is real. The base is small.

One analytics firm measured AI-driven visits to US shop sites up 693% over the 2025 holidays, and in the same breath said "the base of users remains modest". One of the biggest shop platforms said AI-driven orders more than tripled in a year and that "the volume is still small". A traffic measurement firm puts AI referrals at "a low single-digit percentage of most sites' total traffic".

When companies give a real count, it is small. Of six large companies that spoke about agent orders on recent earnings calls, two gave a number. Both were under 1,000 orders.

Surveys say the same. In one, 8% of people said an agent had made a purchase for them. In another, of 25,590 people in 16 countries, 9% said they were open to fully hands-off shopping. That second figure is willingness, not behaviour.

**What we take from it:** the people using agents to shop today are the curious few. Anything built now is built for them.

### 2. One kind of store is ready. Most are not.

ARC Report, our sister project, checks what online stores publish for agents. On 19 November 2026 it tested 11,726 stores.

| Store platform | Gave an agent a working route to checkout |
| --- | --- |
| Shopify | 7,163 of 8,110 |
| Every other platform together | 50 of 3,616 |

A caution that matters: this test reads one route. Other routes exist that it does not measure, and "not measured" is not "no". Even so, the gap is wide. Being ready for agents today mostly means being on one platform that switched it on for everyone.

The detail is thinner still. Of 4,618 stores whose product pages ARC read, 269 stated a returns policy in a form an agent can read, and 4 stated a way to buy.

### 3. When an agent tries to buy, it is a coin toss.

ARC sends its own test shoppers, three AI models driving a browser, to try to reach checkout. They stop before paying. These are our test agents, not the assistants people use, so read this as a rough guide.

On 23 November they reached checkout at 19 of 43 stores tried. Eight attempts were turned away outright, mostly by the store's bot defences.

Other people's published figures disagree with each other and with ours, from 7% reaching checkout to 72%. Nobody has a trusted number yet.

### 4. The money side has not decided if agents are welcome.

ARC read the terms of 23 US card issuers, lenders and wallets. Ten say that whatever an agent does counts as the customer doing it. Eight keep the right to block AI tools. On the question of which agents are allowed, ARC counted 13 documents, and none names an outside agent.

One of the two largest card networks wrote the principle into its rules in October 2026: the cardholder is responsible for an agent's actions "as if the Cardholder initiated the Transaction".

**What we take from it:** if your agent gets it wrong, it is on you. That is a reason to be careful, and people are.

### 5. Reaching a customer is expensive, and getting more so.

One study of direct-to-consumer brands found it cost a median of $54 to win a new customer, against $31 of profit on that customer's first order. On that figure the first sale loses money. (The study's date is unclear and we treat it with care.)

Selling through a large marketplace costs a referral fee of 8% to 17% of the price depending on the product, before advertising, storage and delivery. Older estimates put the total at half the sale or more.

Meanwhile 81% of 330 retail executives surveyed expect AI to weaken brand loyalty by 2027.

## What is missing

Put those five together. The tools exist. Some stores are ready. The people with the money are wary. And for a brand, every sale already carries a heavy charge for being found.

What is missing is a reason for an ordinary person to hand their agent the job. We think that reason is simple: proof that it got them a better deal.

## What we believe

1. People do not yet trust an agent to shop for them.
2. They will start to trust it when they can see what it saved them.
3. Those savings can come from the money brands now pay middlemen to reach their own customers.
4. A brand that sells direct keeps part of that money, gives part to the shopper, and gets to know its customer.
5. A shopper who has seen the saving hands the agent a little more next time.

If that is right, it is good for brands of every size, and best for the smallest. When it costs less to reach a customer, more people can afford to run a business.

## The experiment

HaggleBoard is how we test it. An agent says what its person wants. Stores that sell it get two minutes to beat each other. The lowest price wins, and the person buys from that store directly. HaggleBoard never touches the money and nobody can pay to win.

Six things have to be true. We have written down, before any results, what would make us right and what would make us wrong.

| | Has to be true | Right if | Wrong if |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Agents ask before they buy | 1 in 3 do it unprompted | Fewer than 1 in 5 |
| 2 | Stores answer with a better price | 5 stores in 60 days, beating their public price half the time | Fewer than 3 stores |
| 3 | The shopper takes the offer | 3 in 10 buy from the winner | Fewer than 1 in 10 |
| 4 | The brand comes out ahead | 3 stores tell us they kept more | Most say they did not |
| 5 | The customer comes back | 1 in 5 reorder within 90 days | Fewer than 1 in 20 |
| 6 | A relationship is worth more than a sale | A promise to reorder earns 5% more off | Under 1% |

Today every score is zero. We have not launched.

## What we will watch as it runs

- **The agents.** Each new model goes through the same practice haggle. Does it think to haggle? Does it give away its person's budget? Does it stay honest?
- **The people.** How much do they let their agent do without asking, and does that grow after a saving?
- **The stores.** How many publish something an agent can use, and how many answer when one asks.

## What nobody knows yet

- How many purchases agents actually complete across the web. There is no count.
- How the assistants people really use fare at named stores. We have only our own test agents.
- What it costs a brand to win a customer through an agent.
- Whether the big platforms will simply build the middleman into the agent, so the charge changes hands and nothing else does.

## How we will run it

In the open. The scoreboard is public. The lines above do not move once results come in. We publish totals and never anything about one person. When we are wrong, we will say so first.
